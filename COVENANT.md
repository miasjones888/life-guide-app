# life-guide-app — Compiled Design Thinking

*All responses from design conversation, April 14 2026. For use as context in Claude Code.*

---

## What Should This App Never Do

Given everything about how you're built and what you've said about your own patterns, here's what it should never do:

**Never ask you to make decisions at the wrong moment.** A life management app that surfaces choices — "which project do you want to work on?" — at the point of entry is an initiation killer. The decision about what to do should happen during the weekly setup, on paper, in advance. The app should reflect a decision already made, not prompt you to make one in real time.

**Never show you everything at once.** The full project universe visible simultaneously is the anxiety machine. The weight of all open loops at once is exactly what contributes to freeze. It should show you what's relevant now, not the whole board.

**Never gamify.** Streaks, points, completion percentages — all of this works against you specifically. Gamification creates a secondary failure state: now you've failed the task AND broken the streak. It also turns rest and low-output periods into visible losses, which for someone with depression is actively harmful.

**Never moralize about consistency.** No "you haven't logged in in 5 days" notifications. No implicit or explicit pressure around regularity. The app should be there when you open it and neutral about when that is.

**Never make adding things easier than finishing them.** If the friction to capture a new idea is lower than the friction to mark something done or closed, the app will fill up with open loops and become another source of weight. Intake should have just enough friction to require intention.

**Never require a long session to be useful.** If it only pays off after 20 minutes of setup or review, you won't use it on hard days — which are exactly the days you need it most. Every interaction should have a useful output in under two minutes if necessary.

**Never flatten the difference between project types.** A creative writing fragment, a build project, a research thread, and a curriculum are not the same kind of thing. An app that treats them identically loses the information that makes the distinction meaningful — what "next step" means is completely different for each.

**Never simulate urgency that isn't real.** Due dates, countdowns, overdue flags — these should only appear for things with actual external deadlines. Artificial urgency on creative work is just anxiety generation.

**Never be the place where meaning-making happens.** The app is infrastructure. Your thinking, your connections, your interpretation of your own work — that happens in the notebook, in your head, in conversation. The app holds the containers. It does not fill them.

The one underlying principle behind all of these: it should reduce the cost of returning after a gap, not punish you for the gap existing.

## What Should This App Always Do

**Always show you the one next move.** Not a task list. Not a project overview. The single most actionable next step for whatever is active. You've already decided what's active during weekly setup — the app just needs to surface what that means today, concretely.

**Always remember where you were.** When you open a project after a gap, it should reconstruct last known state without you having to do any mental archaeology. What was happening, what the next step was, what decisions were pending. The cost of returning should be near zero.

**Always distinguish between capture and action.** There needs to be a frictionless place to put things — ideas, fragments, observations, links — that is clearly separate from the space where work happens. Capture without commitment. The inbox and the workbench are not the same room.

**Always use language that matches how you actually think about your own life.** Care language over task language. "Tending" instead of "completing." "Dormant" instead of "overdue." The vocabulary of the app shapes how you relate to your own work. It should feel like your system, not a productivity template someone else designed.

**Always make the current load visible without judgment.** Three active slots, the parking lot, the ideas box. The shape of the whole without the weight of it. Knowing what's parked is different from feeling crushed by it — the app should make the former possible without triggering the latter.

**Always support short sessions.** Whatever you can do in 5 minutes should feel complete. Log a fragment, mark a move done, capture an idea, check what's next. The app should be fully useful at low energy, not just when you have capacity for a long session.

**Always preserve research context with the project it belongs to.** When you return to a project, the research you did for it should be there, attached, organized in the order you'll need it. Not in a separate tool, not in a folder somewhere else, right there inside the project.

**Always reflect the actual hierarchy.** Job search is highest urgency right now. The three active slots are the work. The parking lot is real but not present. The app should make that hierarchy legible at a glance — not treat everything as equally weighted.

**Always have a clear distinction between active and parked.** The moment something moves to the parking lot, it should feel genuinely set down, not just pushed to the bottom of a list. Out of view until a slot opens. That cognitive release is the whole point.

**Always support the weekly setup ritual.** The Sunday reset is the decision-making moment. The app should have a specific mode or surface for this — reviewing what's alive, naming the two non-negotiables, writing one move per active project, choosing the deep focus. The weekly setup is where the whole system gets its direction. The app should make that ritual low-friction and complete.

**Always be honest about what's actually in it.** No optimistic auto-organization, no inferred structure you didn't put there. If something is a fragment with no clear next step, it should look like that. The app should reflect reality, not aspirationally tidy it.

The underlying principle: it should feel like a second brain that respects how your actual brain works — not a productivity system designed for someone else that you're trying to adapt yourself to fit.
